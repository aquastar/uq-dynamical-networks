Part 0 · What uncertainty quantification is

A number, a spread, a check, a decision

How sure are we, and what does it cost to be wrong? Uncertainty quantification is the second and third panel: turn the number into a spread, check the spread against what actually happened, then decide with it.

Four beats. The first panel is what most forecasts, dashboards and papers give. The second is what the question deserves; the numbers are the ward’s own, computed from the 2000 runs of Part 1. The third is why anyone bothers: a decision, and the check that the spread was honest. Dr. Chen’s one-line definition of UQ is the caption; say it slowly. Part 0 · What uncertainty quantification is

Two kinds of uncertainty, and only one of them shrinks

Spread = luck + not knowing. In symbols, Var Z = E[Var(Z | β)] + Var(E[Z | β]). Data shrinks only the second term; the first is the floor under every forecast.

Der Kiureghian & Ditlevsen 2009, Structural Safety (aleatory or epistemic? does it matter?) · Hüllermeier & Waegeman 2021, Machine Learning (the two kinds in machine learning)

Three beats. Left: aleatoric, the randomness that stays when you know everything. Right: epistemic, the part that is only ignorance, so data can shrink it. The law of total variance in the caption is the only formula of Part 0; it says the two add up. On the ladder two slides from now, rung 1 is the left panel and rungs 2 to 5 are all versions of the right one.

Luck

the randomness you can’t remove

“Suppose we knew everything about the virus and the ward. How big will it get?”

The one theorem. A quantile estimated from n runs is off by about √(p(1−p)/n) in probability: from 2000 runs, the 5% and 95% edges of our band are good to about one person (Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz).

Read the question aloud. It is what the whole part answers.

Numbers we don’t know

learn β from ten days of data

“It’s day 10. We’ve seen the case counts so far. How contagious is this thing, and what happens next?”

The one theorem. As the tolerance shrinks to zero, the kept guesses are draws from the exact posterior p(β | counts); with a tolerance of four, from a posterior given counts measured to within four (Pritchard et al. 1999; Beaumont, Zhang & Balding 2002).

3

A graph we don’t fully know

the badges missed a third of the contacts

“The badges recorded the contacts. But badges miss things. How wrong does that make us?”

The one theorem. Averaging the forecast over graphs drawn from p(graph | badges) gives the posterior predictive, and its 90% band is honest exactly when that graph distribution is: a wrong prior over missed contacts is a wrong band (Bayes; Génois et al. 2015 for the missed-contact rate).

Tracking what we can’t see

who is infected right now

“We test 30% of the ward every two days. Tests miss 10% of cases. Who is infected right now?”

The one theorem. With K copies the filter’s estimate converges to the true posterior at rate 1/√K (Del Moral 2004), but the K needed grows exponentially with the dimension of what is observed: that is why 1000 copies collapse on a city (Snyder et al. 2008).

Part 5 of 8 · 13 min

5

Guarantees for any model

fix a model’s band in three lines

“Any model can say 90% and deliver less. Can we fix the band without touching the model?”

The one theorem. If the n calibration cases and the new case are exchangeable, then P(truth ∈ forecast ± q) lies between 90% and 90% + 1/(n+1), for any forecaster and any distribution (Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer 2005; Lei et al. 2018).

Part 5 · Guarantees for any model

The 3-line recipe

1Take any forecaster. Keep a few hundred past cases it was not trained on: the calibration cases.
2On those, measure how far off it was: |truth − forecast|. Take the 90th percentile. Call it q.
3From now on, report forecast ± q.
The promise: the truth falls inside forecast ± q at least 90% of the time, on average over calibration sets, for any forecaster, with no assumption about the model. A set of 2000 cases wobbles by about a point. One condition: future cases must be exchangeable with the calibration cases: deal the calibration cases out as cards, add tomorrow’s, shuffle; if nobody could tell which card was tomorrow’s, the promise holds. Its name: conformal prediction.

Angelopoulos & Bates 2023 (gentle intro, with code) · Shafer & Vovk 2008

Before the note appears, ask the room: “what’s the catch?” Then say the condition twice. That is where networks and time make trouble.

Part 5 · Guarantees for any model

Why graphs and time break the promise, and the fixes

Neighbours are not independent

Nurse 12’s case tells you about nurse 13’s.

The graph changes

Fix: calibrate for the graph you will have at prediction time.

Time drifts

A variant arrives. Policy changes.

Fix: adjust q online whenever coverage slips, like a thermostat. Restored the coverage of the CDC ensemble’s COVID intervals.

Running it backwards

forward is done: now the same three lines, backwards

“It’s day 8. We have a good picture of who is infected. Who started it?”

The one theorem. On a regular tree, the person with the most orderings of infection consistent with the picture is the maximum-likelihood source, and is found in linear time; on a general graph the likelihood needs simulation or message passing (Shah & Zaman 2011).

Deciding anyway

before anyone is sick, so patient zero is unknown too

“We have 8 doses, before the next outbreak. Whom do we vaccinate?”

The one theorem. The expected number of people saved by a set of doses is monotone and submodular, so greedy picking is within 1 − 1/e ≈ 63% of the best set (Nemhauser, Wolsey & Fisher 1978; Kempe, Kleinberg & Tardos 2003); the bad-day version is not submodular, and the guarantee weakens (Ohsaka & Yoshida 2017).

Closing the loop: a set in, a guarantee out

Part 5 gave a band or a set that holds the truth 90% of the time

Optimise against that set, and the decision inherits the 90%.

Same for Part 6

Isolate everyone in the 90% suspect set and, if the posterior is calibrated, you caught patient zero 9 times in 10. Ours wasn’t quite: Part 5’s point.

Newer idea

Train the forecaster for decision quality, not accuracy.

Isolating the 90% suspect set caught patient zero in 83% of outbreaks and isolated 23.5 people on average. The most-connected-8 plan predicted a bad day of 52 from 400 simulated outbreaks; in 100 more, re-simulated with fresh random numbers, it was 53: the same simulator checked against itself, which is all a decision made before the outbreak can be checked against.

Part 8 · Open challenges

Where the ward stops and the research starts

Structure and dynamics that shape each other

Our graph was fixed. In real systems the outbreak changes the contacts: isolation, panic, line trips. Joint uncertainty over both is mostly open.

Gross, D’Lima & Blasius 2006 PRL (adaptive networks) · Holme & Saramäki 2012 (temporal networks)

A million nodes, streaming data

Particle filters collapse, posteriors need surrogates, graphs cannot be resampled. Local filters, neural posteriors and calibrated surrogates are the current answers, and none of them reports its own error bars yet.

Rebeschini & van Handel 2015 · Psaros et al. 2023 J. Comp. Phys. (UQ in scientific ML)

Guarantees without exchangeability

Conformal on graphs and under drift is two years old. The bands are honest in experiments; the theory covers only special cases, and training a model for the decision it feeds, with a guarantee on the decision, has four papers.

Lunde, Levina & Zhu 2025 JASA · Gibbs & Candès 2021 · Barber et al. 2023 · Hu et al. 2026 ICLR
